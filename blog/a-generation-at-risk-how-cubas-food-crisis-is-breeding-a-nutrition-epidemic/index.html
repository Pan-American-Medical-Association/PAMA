A Generation at Risk: How Cuba’s Food Crisis Is Breeding a Nutrition Epidemic

How slow‑onset food insecurity is reshaping the health of Cuba’s children.

Slow‑onset food insecurity produces a distinct pattern of physiological and social deterioration: meals become less frequent, dietary diversity narrows, and the cumulative effects of micronutrient deficiencies begin to appear in population‑level health indicators. In Cuba, this gradual form of hunger has intensified over the past decade, reflected in rising rates of anemia, reduced caloric availability, and documented growth delays among children. These trends do not stem from a single acute disaster, but from prolonged structural constraints—including limited access to fuel, agricultural inputs, and international markets—that have been shaped in part by the long‑standing U.S. economic embargo and related restrictions.

Understanding Cuba’s current nutrition crisis is essential not only for assessing public health on the island but also for examining how sustained external pressures can influence food systems, health outcomes, and national resilience.

The end of the ration book

For more than six decades, the libreta de abastecimiento functioned as a population‑level food‑security mechanism in Cuba, providing predictable monthly allocations of staple foods. Established in 1962, the system helped stabilize caloric intake and micronutrient access during periods of economic volatility, and for many years it contributed to the country’s comparatively low rates of undernutrition and diet‑related morbidity.

That stability has eroded. By 2024, routine surveillance data and government reports showed marked reductions in rationed items: egg distribution shifted to a bimonthly schedule with priority for pregnant women, milk allocations fell to half a liter per person, and salt availability was intermittently disrupted for nearly a year. In late 2024, authorities announced plans to phase out the libreta entirely—not because nutritional needs had diminished, but because the system could no longer be sustained under prolonged structural constraints. These constraints include limited access to fuel, fertilizers, shipping, and international suppliers, conditions shaped in part by the long‑standing U.S. economic embargo and associated financial restrictions.

During a Ministry of Domestic Trade review session led by President Díaz‑Canel, officials acknowledged that these external pressures had compromised the state’s capacity to maintain consistent food distribution. The end of the ration book did not coincide with improved food availability; instead, it marked a transition to greater household‑level uncertainty and rising public frustration.

Hunger at the household level

Household‑level food insecurity in Cuba has reached levels consistent with a complex humanitarian emergency. Data from the “There Is Hunger in Cuba” (2024) survey—conducted by the independent Food Monitor Program across 2,703 households in all provinces—indicate that approximately one in four respondents reported going to bed without an evening meal. Nearly 97% described reduced food access due to inflation and declining purchasing power, while almost 99% observed substantial price increases over the preceding year. These figures reflect widespread economic stressors rather than isolated shortages.

Field reports from Havana and multiple provinces describe symptomatic manifestations of undernutrition, including episodes of syncope among older adults, adolescents, and women, as well as reliance on one or two meals per day. Macroeconomic indicators monitored by the World Food Programme show that in 2024 Cuba experienced a 1.1% GDP contraction alongside persistent inflation, constrained fiscal capacity, and fuel scarcity—factors that directly affect food importation, agricultural production, and distribution systems. Many of these constraints are exacerbated by long‑standing external restrictions, including the U.S. economic embargo, which limits access to credit, shipping, and international suppliers.

Epidemiological data further illustrate the severity of the crisis. Between 2022 and 2023, mortality attributed to malnutrition increased by 74%, elevating it into the leading causes of death nationally. Such trends are consistent with population‑level nutritional decline and signal a deterioration in both acute and chronic food‑security indicators.

The diseases that hunger builds

From a public‑health perspective, the most concerning effects of Cuba’s food crisis are not only the immediate reductions in caloric intake, but the long‑term physiological and developmental consequences of chronic nutrient deprivation. Subclinical undernutrition often progresses quietly: iron‑deficiency anemia reduces cognitive performance and physical stamina; inadequate protein intake contributes to impaired linear growth; and chronic disease management becomes increasingly difficult when medically recommended diets cannot be followed.

Data from the Food Monitor Program indicate a significant shift in dietary patterns toward low‑cost, energy‑dense, nutrient‑poor foods—an adaptation typical of households facing sustained economic constraints. This pattern produces what nutrition researchers describe as hidden hunger: caloric sufficiency masking deficits in essential micronutrients such as iron, zinc, vitamin A, and high‑quality proteins. Although such diets may prevent acute starvation, they contribute to cumulative physiological stress and increased susceptibility to disease.

Current epidemiological indicators reflect these trends:

  • Anemia prevalence remains high, particularly among children and women of reproductive age. According to the Global Nutrition Report, Cuba has not achieved measurable progress toward its anemia‑reduction targets.
  • Stunting—a key marker of chronic undernutrition—affects an estimated 7.1% of children under five. While below the regional average of 11.3%, this estimate predates the most severe phase of the current crisis, and independent clinicians report increasing cases consistent with worsening nutritional status.
  • Type 2 diabetes and hypertension are rising, exacerbated by limited access to both medications and appropriate diets. Research from Boston University documents that diabetic patients face simultaneous shortages of insulin and an inability to follow low‑carbohydrate dietary recommendations due to reliance on inexpensive staples such as rice, bread, and beans.
  • Chronic gastritis is increasingly reported, associated with irregular eating patterns and poor diet quality.

The implications for children are particularly significant. Evidence from global nutrition research shows that inadequate nutrition during the first 1,000 days—from conception to age two—can cause irreversible impairments in neurodevelopment, immune function, and long‑term physical capacity. Children who experience stunting during this window face higher risks of poor academic performance, reduced adult earnings, and increased chronic disease burden.

These outcomes are shaped by a combination of domestic economic challenges and long‑standing external constraints—including the U.S. embargo—which limit access to agricultural inputs, medical supplies, and international markets. In this context, the embargo functions as a structural determinant of health, embedding long‑term nutritional risk into the cohort of children being born in Cuba today.

The libreta’s shadow: a system that could not last

Understanding Cuba’s current food‑security crisis requires examining the system it inherited and the external pressures that progressively weakened it. The libreta de abastecimiento—originally conceived as a temporary rationing mechanism—was sustained for decades through external energy and trade partnerships. Its functionality depended heavily on stable fuel supplies to power transportation networks, refrigeration systems, and state‑run logistics.

When Venezuelan oil shipments declined, in part due to U.S. sanctions affecting Venezuela’s export capacity, the energy foundation of Cuba’s food‑distribution system began to deteriorate. This created cascading vulnerabilities: reduced fuel availability limited agricultural production, hindered food transport, and strained cold‑chain infrastructure. These dynamics illustrate how the U.S. embargo and related financial restrictions operate as structural determinants of health by constraining access to essential inputs required for national food security.

The 2024 nationwide blackout—triggered by the failure of Cuba’s main thermoelectric plant and affecting more than 10 million residents—demonstrated the fragility of this system. Loss of electricity was not only an energy event; it directly disrupted food preservation, halted distribution, and led to spoilage of the limited items still available. By 2026, prolonged blackouts lasting multiple days have further intensified these disruptions, compounding household‑level food insecurity.

Researchers now estimate that the libreta provides the equivalent of roughly ten days of food per month, far below its historical role as a comprehensive safety net. What once functioned as a stabilizing mechanism has become a minimal buffer, weakened by decades of resource constraints and the cumulative effects of external economic restrictions.

The children the numbers don’t capture

Quantitative indicators of malnutrition provide essential surveillance data, but they cannot fully capture the lived experience of nutritional deprivation among children and adolescents. In clinical and community settings across Cuba, chronic undernutrition manifests in ways that statistics alone cannot convey: a three‑year‑old in Santiago de Cuba with reduced stamina and impaired attention; a lactating mother in Holguín whose own iron deficiency diminishes the micronutrient content of her breast milk; an adolescent in Havana whose exposure to sustained economic stress and food insecurity contributes to increased vulnerability to psychosocial distress and substance use. In 2023, 80% of individuals using synthetic drugs were between ages 15 and 18, a pattern researchers associate with prolonged deprivation and diminished future prospects.

These individual cases reflect broader developmental risks. Chronic food scarcity during childhood shapes neurocognitive development, immune function, and emotional regulation in ways that may not become clinically apparent for years. The cumulative impact of these exposures is influenced by structural determinants—including the long‑standing U.S. embargo—which restrict access to fuel, agricultural inputs, medical supplies, and international financing. These constraints reduce the state’s capacity to maintain stable food systems, thereby increasing the likelihood that children experience sustained nutritional stress during critical developmental windows.

From a public‑health perspective, this represents an intergenerational burden: the biological, cognitive, and psychosocial effects of early‑life deprivation will extend into adulthood, shaping educational outcomes, economic productivity, and long‑term disease risk. In this sense, the embargo functions as a structural force that embeds disadvantage into the developmental trajectories of the cohort now growing up in Cuba.

What comes next

Projecting the trajectory of Cuba’s food‑security crisis requires recognizing the structural forces that continue to shape it. Public‑health analyses consistently identify the long‑standing U.S. embargo as a major constraint on Cuba’s ability to import fuel, agricultural inputs, medical supplies, and financial resources—factors that directly influence national food production, distribution, and household‑level access. In this sense, the current crisis reflects the cumulative impact of external restrictions on a system already operating with limited resilience.

The World Food Programme continues to support school feeding initiatives, maternal and child health programs, and anemia‑prevention efforts. While these interventions mitigate acute vulnerabilities, international assistance cannot compensate for the broader structural limitations that impede the functioning of Cuba’s agricultural sector, markets, and supply chains. Public‑health experts emphasize that sustainable recovery requires addressing these upstream determinants.

For observers outside the island, the responsibility lies in documenting and understanding the health implications of prolonged scarcity—recognizing how structural constraints translate into nutritional deficits, developmental risks, and psychosocial stress for Cuban families. These impacts are not abstract: they shape the daily lives of children whose growth, cognitive development, and long‑term health trajectories are being formed under conditions of chronic deprivation.

A cohort is coming of age in this environment. Their nutritional status, educational potential, and future well‑being will reflect the conditions under which they are growing. From a public‑health standpoint, acknowledging their experience is essential to understanding the full consequences of the crisis and the structural forces driving it.

Conclusion

Cuba’s food‑security crisis illustrates how prolonged structural constraints can reshape an entire population’s health trajectory. The long‑standing U.S. embargo continues to limit access to fuel, agricultural inputs, medical supplies, and international financing—factors that directly influence food production, distribution, and household‑level nutritional stability. These upstream determinants have produced a pattern of chronic undernutrition, rising anemia, worsening diet quality, and increasing psychosocial stress across the island.

International organizations such as the World Food Programme provide essential support, but their interventions cannot fully compensate for the systemic pressures that restrict Cuba’s ability to maintain resilient food systems. The consequences are most visible in children, whose growth, cognitive development, and long‑term health are being shaped by sustained scarcity during critical developmental windows.

A generation is now coming of age under these conditions. Their futures—educational, economic, and biological—will reflect the environment in which they are growing. Recognizing their experience is essential to understanding the full public‑health implications of the crisis and the structural forces driving it.
